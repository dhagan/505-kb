High-performance double-handers like the 49er, Flying Dutchman (FD), and 470 share the 505's exact slow-speed problem: oversized mainsails with long booms, high-aspect foils that stall easily, and crews on the wire whose weight swings radically alters boat balance.
Olympic and world-class campaigns in these classes use specific, repeatable single-boat drills to master line control, hovering, and acceleration. Here is how to adapt their best drills directly to a 505 in 8–12 knots:

1. From the 49er: The "Rudderless Box" & Scull-Free Hover

49er sailors cannot scull the rudder to hold a spot—the blade is too small and stalls immediately, and any sudden weight shift capsizes the boat. They train station-keeping purely through sail balance and heel angle.
• The Drill: Drop two small buoys 1.5 boat lengths apart (side-by-side, perpendicular to the wind) to create a narrow "box." Enter the box on starboard, stop completely, and hold position for 30 seconds without touching the tiller more than 5 degrees.
• 505 Application (8–12 Knots):
	• Heel-to-Steer: Instead of turning the rudder when the bow starts to fall off, the crew shifts 6 inches inboard (creating slight weather heel to bring the bow back up). If the bow heads up too close to head-to-wind, the crew steps onto the leeward floor/tank edge to induce leeward heel, naturally carving the bow back down to a 45-degree luff.
	• Main/Jib Opposition: The helm holds the boom out at ~45 degrees with zero vang (acting as a brake/weather-vane), while the crew pulses the jib sheet in and out 6 inches to keep the bow locked onto the windward buoy.

2. From the Flying Dutchman (FD): "Genoa-First" Sternway & Roll-Acceleration

The FD is the closest cousin to the 505—a trapeze dinghy with massive sail power and a huge overlapping headsail. FD sailors drill backing down and punching out by letting the headsail do 80% of the steering and initial drive so the giant main doesn't trip the boat.
• The Drill: Approach a single mark at full speed on starboard, execute an emergency crash-stop, back down two full boat lengths in a straight line, and immediately accelerate onto a close-hauled port or starboard lane.
• 505 Application (8–12 Knots):
	• The Crash-Stop: At 2 boat lengths from the mark, blow the vang and cunningham completely, push the boom forward past the shrouds, and let the crew back the jib to windward while keeping their weight low on the windward tank.
	• The FD "Roll-Punch" Exit: In 8–12 knots, a 505 doesn't have enough wind to blast straight onto the wire from a dead stop without stalling the centerboard. Borrow the FD exit:
		1. Stop sternway by pulling the jib across to leeward and trimming it to 70% while the main is still completely flogging.
		2. As the jib pulls the bow down, the crew stays inboard on the leeward side for 1.5 seconds to heel the boat 10 degrees to leeward, then hikes/trapezes out smoothly as the helm trims the main. That subtle leeward-to-flat roll pumps water across the centerboard and kicks the boat forward a half-length ahead of a flat-footed start.

3. From the 470: The "30-Second Acceleration Gate" & "No-Go Zone" Escape

470 fleets are notoriously aggressive on the line, requiring micro-adjustments in tight spaces and instant acceleration in marginal trapezing conditions (8–12 knots).
• The Drill (The Acceleration Gate): Set two buoys 3 boat lengths apart (one windward, one leeward) to simulate the exact width of a starting gap. Practice entering the gap late, hovering, and hitting full speed before crossing the line between them.
• 505 Application (8–12 Knots):
	• The "Two-Gear" Sheet Trim: 470 helms never pull the main straight to its upwind mark in 8–12 knots. When punching out of your hover:
		• Gear 1 (0–3 seconds): Trim the main only until the top batten stops flogging (boom over the quarter, vang still loose). Keep the centerboard 2 inches up to reduce wetted surface drag while building speed.
		• Gear 2 (3–5 seconds): Once boat speed hits ~5 knots and the crew is fully extended on the wire (or hiking hard at 8 knots), drop the last 2 inches of board, pull the vang to your 10-knot mark, and sheet the main to the centerline.
	• The "No-Go" Escape Drill: Intentionally let the boat drift head-to-wind and get stuck in irons (which happens constantly when waiting for a rabbit to pass). Practice breaking out without losing more than half a boat length to leeward: Crew backs the jib hard to windward and leans to windward to heel the boat, while the helm pushes the tiller away from the desired tack and holds the boom forward—the exact second the bow passes 30 degrees off the wind, snap the jib across and flatten the boat.

4. Combined Routine: The "Olympic 20-Minute Loop"

Combine all three into a high-intensity solo drill block:
1. Minutes 0–5 (49er Hover): Hold position inside a 1.5-boat-length box using only heel and jib trim (tiller locked).
2. Minutes 5–10 (FD Crash-Stop & Back): Reach in at full speed, crash-stop at a buoy, back down 2 lengths using the boom and backed jib, and execute the leeward-to-flat roll punch.
3. Minutes 10–20 (470 Gate Starts): Run back-to-back 45-second starting sequences through a 3-boat-length gate, alternating between a hovering start (simulating waiting for the rabbit) and a dip-and-hook start (simulating timing the pathfinder at full pace).
